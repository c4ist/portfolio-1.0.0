Every interface has a volume. Some products shout: gradients on every surface, a badge on every nav item, an animation on every hover. Others say what they need to say and then get out of the way.

I keep coming back to the quiet ones, and not for aesthetic reasons. Volume is a budget. Every element that competes for attention spends a little of the user's, and there is no overdraft facility.

## Where the noise comes from

Noise is rarely a single decision. It accumulates:

- A separator line gets added because two groups feel too close, instead of doubling the space between them.
- A colour gets reused decoratively, so the colour that used to mean "you can click this" now means nothing in particular.
- An entrance animation gets attached to a hover state, so a 300ms flourish plays forty times a minute.

None of these is wrong on its own. Together they turn a page into a room where everyone is talking.

## The cheapest fixes

Space first. Negative space is the only grouping tool that adds no visual weight, and the rule is simple: the gap between groups should be at least twice the gap inside a group. Most layouts that reach for a divider only needed another eight pixels.

One colour, one meaning. If your link colour also decorates an icon and tints a chart series, it has stopped being a signal. Give the decorative uses a neutral and keep the signal.

Motion where it is rare. A staged entrance on a page you visit once a day is a gift. The same motion on a button you press two hundred times is a tax.

## What quiet is not

Quiet is not featureless, and it is not grey. Restraint is only interesting when there is something to restrain: a single accent that lands hard because nothing else is competing, one serif italic in a page of sans-serif, one surface that lifts because everything else sits flat.

The goal is not less design. It is design you notice on the second look rather than the first.
